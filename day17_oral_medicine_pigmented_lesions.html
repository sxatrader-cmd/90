Foundations · How to Think About Oral Pigmentation

Melanin biology: where is melanin made and how does it reach keratinocytes?BASICS

Melanin is the brown–black pigment responsible for most oral pigmented lesions. It is produced inside organelles called melanosomes within dendritic cells called melanocytes, which sit in the basal cell layer of the epithelium (roughly one melanocyte per 5–10 basal keratinocytes).

Pigment-loaded melanosomes travel along the dendritic processes and are transferred to neighboring keratinocytes — the melanocyte "feeds" pigment to its neighbors. This functional unit is the epidermal melanin unit.

Why it matters: most forms of oral hyperpigmentation (physiologic, drug-induced, addisonian, melasma…) share the mechanism of increased basilar melanin synthesis WITHOUT an increase in the number of melanocytes. Memorize that phrase — it repeats across almost every entity below.

Melanocyte numbers are similar across ethnic groups; skin-color differences reflect melanosome size, activity, and distribution — not melanocyte count.

Increased melanin synthesis vs. melanocytic proliferation — why does the distinction matter?CONCEPT

This single distinction classifies almost every pigmented lesion:

  • Increased synthesis: normal melanocyte number, more pigment produced. Examples: ephelis, melanotic macule, physiologic, drug-induced, smoker's, melasma, Addison's. Microscopy: basilar hyperpigmentation, no increase in melanocyte count.
  • Proliferation: an actual increase in the number of melanocytes/nevus cells. Examples: melanocytic nevus (benign) and malignant melanoma (malignant).
Practical rule Synthesis-based lesions are usually benign and may fade once the trigger is removed. Proliferation-based lesions require histologic evaluation because melanoma belongs here. Any new, changing, or unexplained focal pigmented lesion → biopsy.
What is "melanin incontinence"?TERM

Melanin incontinence = leakage of melanin granules from the basal layer down into the connective tissue, where they are engulfed by macrophages called melanophages. Microscopically it appears as free melanin in the lamina propria.

It is prominent in: drug-induced pigmentation, postinflammatory hyperpigmentation, pigmented lichen planus, and addisonian pigmentation. It signals that pigment has "dropped" out of its normal location and usually accompanies basilar hyperpigmentation.

Practical diagnostic approach to any oral pigmented lesion?APPROACH
  1. Focal or diffuse/multifocal? A solitary focal lesion raises concern for melanoma → low threshold to biopsy. Diffuse points toward systemic/drug/racial causes.
  2. Endogenous (melanin/blood) or exogenous (metal/graphite/ink)? Amalgam and graphite are gray–black, often near an obvious source.
  3. Present since childhood or new? New diffuse pigmentation appearing de novo in an adult is a red flag for a pathologic cause (not physiologic).
  4. Drug & systemic history: antimalarials, phenothiazines, contraceptives; symptoms of Addison/Cushing/thyroid disease; smoking/alcohol.
  5. General exam: cutaneous bronzing, jaundice, perioral macules, axillary freckling, iris nodules.
  6. When is biopsy mandatory? Persistent solitary lesion, sudden appearance, change in color/size, ulceration, or no clear explanation.
Golden principle The differential for a focal pigmented lesion always includes: melanotic macule, nevus, amalgam tattoo, melanoma. When in doubt, biopsy decides.

Part 1 · Malignant Melanoma (continued)

Clinicopathologic subtypes & growth phases

What are the four main clinicopathologic subtypes of melanoma?SUBTYPES
  • Superficial spreading melanoma — most common cutaneous type; spreads horizontally first.
  • Lentigo maligna melanoma — chronically sun-exposed areas in older patients.
  • Acral lentiginous melanoma — palms, soles, subungual; the most common type in dark-skinned and Asian individuals.
  • Nodular melanoma — the only one that begins directly in the vertical growth phase, hence the most aggressive.

In the first three, growth begins as a radial growth phase (lateral, superficial spread) and may later convert to a vertical phase.

Link to the mouth Oral melanoma is not precisely classified into these four cutaneous subtypes, but understanding growth phases (superficial vs. vertical) is what governs prognosis at every site.
Explain radial vs. vertical growth phase and their prognostic meaning.GROWTH

Radial growth phase: tumor cells spread laterally and superficially within the epithelium and superficial connective tissue. Good prognosis if detected and treated before nodules appear.

Vertical growth phase: deep invasion into connective tissue. Clinically heralded by the development of nodularity in a previously macular (flat) lesion — a classic warning sign.

Core idea: depth (the vertical phase) is the gateway to vascular/lymphatic spread, so depth directly drives survival (see Breslow, next card).

Prognosis of melanoma

Which criteria determine melanoma prognosis, and what factors predict a poor outcome?PROGNOSIS
  • Breslow's tumor thickness — depth in mm; the strongest, most precise criterion.
  • Clark's level of invasion — anatomic level of invasion (I–V); classic but less precise than Breslow.
Poor-prognosis factors (memorize):
  • Surface ulceration.
  • Vascular or lymphatic invasion.
  • Neurotropism.
  • High mitotic index.
  • Absence of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes.
  • Clinical: site, age > 60, male sex, and regional/distant metastasis.
Critical number Five-year survival for metastatic melanoma is less than 15%.

Oral (mucosal) malignant melanoma

Epidemiology of primary oral/mucosal melanoma?EPIDEMIOLOGY

Primary mucosal melanomas make up less than 1% of all melanomas. Most develop in the head and neck, mainly the sinonasal tract and oral cavity.

  • Higher prevalence among black-skinned and Japanese people.
  • More frequent in males than females.
  • The etiology of oral melanoma is unknown — unlike the cutaneous variant with clear UV risk factors.
  • Can arise at any age but most present over 50.
  • The palate is the single most common site, followed by the maxillary gingiva / alveolar crest.

Why it is diagnosed late: often asymptomatic early, sits in areas the patient cannot inspect, and may be amelanotic — delaying detection until the vertical phase.

Describe the clinical appearance of oral melanoma (including amelanosis).CLINICAL

No distinctive single appearance. It may be macular, plaque-like, or mass-forming; well-circumscribed or irregular; brown, blue, or black; focal or diffuse.

Beware Up to one-third of oral melanomas show little or no pigmentation (amelanosis) — some tumors mix melanotic and amelanotic areas. Some also present as multifocal pigmentation.
Advanced local signs:
  • Ulceration, pain.
  • Tooth mobility or spontaneous exfoliation.
  • Root resorption, bone loss.
  • Paresthesia/anesthesia.
  • Yet some tumors remain completely asymptomatic.

Broad differential: melanocytic nevus, oral melanotic macule, amalgam tattoo, vascular lesions, other soft-tissue neoplasms. Therefore, biopsy of any persistent solitary pigmented lesion is always warranted.

Prognosis of oral mucosal melanoma specifically?PROGNOSIS

Very poor — worse than cutaneous, due to late diagnosis, the rich vascular/lymphatic mucosa, and difficulty achieving wide surgical margins in the mouth.

  • Five-year survival 15–40%.
  • The palate shows the worst prognosis of intraoral sites.
  • Regional lymphatic metastases are frequent.
  • Fewer than 10% of patients with distant metastases survive 5 years.
  • 10-year survival = 0%.
Diagnosis & management of oral melanoma?MANAGEMENT

Diagnosis: the main clinical/microscopic challenge is deciding whether the lesion is a primary neoplasm or a metastasis from a distant site (lung, skin…). Biopsy and full work-up are done.

Management:

  • For primary tumors: ablative surgery with wide margins is the treatment of choice.
  • Adjuvant radiation may be needed, though its benefit in mucosal melanoma remains unclear.
  • CT / MRI to explore regional lymph-node metastases.
  • Chemo/immunotherapy for metastases or palliation. Interferon-α-2B is approved as adjuvant for primary cutaneous melanoma > 4 mm thick.
Interesting paradox Onset of autoimmunity after immunotherapy (e.g., appearance of vitiligo and autoantibodies) is associated with statistically significant improvement in overall survival in cutaneous melanoma — the immune "attack" on melanocytes signals a good response.

Part 2 · Multifocal / Diffuse Pigmentation

Physiologic (racial) pigmentation

What is physiologic pigmentation and when should it worry you?PHYSIOLOGIC

Physiologic pigmentation is the most common multifocal/diffuse oral pigmentation. Dark-complexioned individuals (blacks, Asians, Latinos) show patchy-to-generalized hyperpigmentation, often on the gingiva but sometimes on other surfaces.

Red flag Pigment is usually first seen in childhood and does not develop de novo in the adult. Sudden or gradual onset of diffuse pigmentation in adulthood — even in darker-skinned patients — should prompt a search for a pathologic cause.

Differential: idiopathic, drug-induced, or smoking-induced melanosis, plus endocrinopathic/systemic disease. History and lab tests are needed.

Microscopy: increased melanin in the basal cell layer, no increase in melanocyte number.

Treatment (cosmetic, if desired): gingivectomy, laser, cryosurgery — but re-pigmentation may eventually recur with all modalities, for unclear reasons.

Drug-induced melanosis

Which drug classes cause melanosis, and what are its features?DRUGS

Drugs cause pigmentation either by soft-tissue deposition of drug metabolites/complexes, or by deposition associated with lipofuscin/iron.

Chief drugs Antimalarials (chloroquine, hydroxychloroquine, quinacrine) — used for autoimmune disease · Phenothiazines (chlorpromazine) · Oral contraceptives · Cytotoxics (cyclophosphamide, busulfan).
  • 10–20% of all acquired melanocytic pigmentation may be drug-induced.
  • Intraorally: diffuse but often localized to one surface (frequently the hard palate), or multifocal. Lesions are flat, no nodularity/swelling.
  • Some drugs cause a specific pattern. Sun exposure may worsen cutaneous drug-induced pigmentation.
Pathology:

Basilar hyperpigmentation and melanin incontinence, without an increase in melanocyte number. Mechanisms are uncertain: drugs/metabolites may stimulate melanogenesis; alternatively chloroquine and chlorpromazine physically bind melanin, and this complexation within melanocytes may contribute to adverse effects.

Diagnosis & course:

If onset can be chronologically tied to the drug (weeks–months), no intervention is needed. It usually fades within a few months after stopping the drug, but hormone-therapy pigmentation may persist longer despite discontinuation. Labs may be needed to rule out an endocrinopathy.

Smoker's melanosis (& alcohol, submucous fibrosis)

Key facts about smoker's melanosis? Is it premalignant?SMOKING
  • Diffuse melanosis seen in some smokers: anterior vestibular maxillary/mandibular gingivae, buccal mucosa, lateral tongue, palate, floor of mouth.
  • Most smokers (even heavy) do NOT show it; it occurs only in certain individuals whose melanin synthesis is stimulated by tobacco smoke products.
  • In dark-skinned individuals with baseline physiologic pigmentation, smoking increases it further.
  • Areas are brown, flat, irregular, sometimes geographic/map-like.
  • Mechanism unknown; likely chemical compounds in cigarettes (rather than the tobacco itself) or the heat of smoke. Smokeless tobacco (snuff) is NOT associated with increased melanosis.
  • Passive smoking in children can increase gingival pigmentation. It rises prominently in the first year of smoking and may fade with reduction.
Exam point Smoker's melanosis is NOT a preneoplastic condition. By contrast, oral submucous fibrosis (from habitual areca/betel-nut chewing, common in East Asia) IS preneoplastic and shows melanosis plus characteristic soft-tissue fibrosis.

Alcohol: also associated with increased oral pigmentation, especially posteriorly (soft palate), and possibly a higher risk of upper aerodigestive tract cancers.

Postinflammatory (inflammatory) hyperpigmentation

What is postinflammatory hyperpigmentation? Link to lichen planus?POST-INFLAM

A well-recognized phenomenon, more common in dark-complexioned individuals, as focal or diffuse pigmentation in areas of previous injury/inflammation. The acne-prone face is a common cutaneous site; in the mouth it is unusual but possible.

  • Rarely, mucosa overlying a non-melanocytic malignancy may become pigmented.
  • Lichen planus: associated pigmentation described across races including Caucasians. Microscopy adds basilar hyperpigmentation and melanin incontinence to the lichenoid features; pigmentation usually subsides as the lesion resolves. Whether it is "postinflammatory" or "inflammatory" remains debated.
  • Spontaneous post-surgical healing pigmentation of palatal donor sites for free gingival grafts has also been reported.

Melasma (Chloasma)

Melasma: distribution, triggers, and treatment?MELASMA

A common acquired symmetric melanosis of sun-exposed skin, frequently the face (forehead, cheeks, upper lip, chin). Distinct female predilection, mostly darker-skinned individuals. Unlike other diffuse melanoses, it evolves rapidly over a few weeks.

Key triggers Sun exposure + hormonal factors (pregnancy and contraceptive hormones). It is the combination of estrogen AND progesterone that induces the pigment; estrogen alone does not. Pregnancy and contraceptives may also cause oral mucosal melanosis.
  • Idiopathic cases: significantly elevated LH in both sexes, with decreased estradiol (women) / testosterone (men).
  • Recent research: the hormonal association is weaker than once believed. Thyroid abnormalities (incl. hypothyroidism) may contribute to pregnancy- and non-pregnancy-associated melasma.
  • Biopsy: basilar melanosis without increased melanocyte number (present ones may be larger). May resolve after parturition or stopping hormones.
Treatment — the triple combination Topical: 4% hydroquinone + 0.05% tretinoin + 0.01% fluocinolone acetonide + photoprotection (SPF 30). Note this is the same triple-combination concept used for melanosis generally.

Part 3 · Melanosis with Systemic / Genetic Disease (Lecture 2)

Addison's disease (hypoadrenocorticism)

Explain the ACTH/α-MSH mechanism of pigmentation in Addison's disease.ADDISON

A potentially life-threatening, under-diagnosed disease. In adults the most common cause is autoimmune — circulating autoantibodies to 21-hydroxylase. Other causes: infection, neoplasia, trauma, drugs, iatrogenic, rarely genetic. The end result is always decreased endogenous corticosteroid.

Mechanism (high-yield):
  1. Low steroid → compensatory ACTH secretion from the anterior pituitary.
  2. Persistent low steroid → loss of feedback inhibition → sustained ACTH.
  3. Serum α-MSH rises concurrently.
Molecular key The precursor gene proopiomelanocortin (POMC) contains both ACTH and α-MSH sequences. During processing they are cleaved independently, but the first 13 amino acids of ACTH are identical to α-MSH. Both stimulate melanocytes → hyperpigmentation.
Clinical:
  • Weakness, vague fatigue, depression — and mucocutaneous hyperpigmentation may be the first sign.
  • Generalized cutaneous bronzing + diffuse patchy oral melanosis are hallmarks. Any oral surface may be affected; oral melanosis may be the first manifestation.
  • Diffuse pigmentation is associated more with chronic than acute-onset disease.
Diagnosis:
  • Requires clinicopathologic correlation; suspect it when oral melanosis accompanies cutaneous bronzing.
  • Oral biopsy: increased basal melanin + melanin incontinence. Differentiate from physiologic and drug-induced.
  • Serum cortisol < 100 nmol/L at 9:00 a.m. is diagnostic of deficiency, with hyponatremia + hyperkalemia.

Treatment: exogenous steroid replacement (gluco- + mineralocorticoids); evidence supports adding adrenal androgens (DHEA) for quality of life. Pigmentation resolves with therapy.

Cushing's syndrome / disease

Cushing's: causes, who gets pigmentation, and diagnostic tests?CUSHING

Results from prolonged exposure to high concentrations of endogenous or exogenous corticosteroids. Most cases are iatrogenic (poorly monitored topical/systemic steroids).

Endogenous causes:
  • Activating pituitary tumor → Cushing's disease.
  • Primary activating adrenal pathology (hyperadrenocorticism).
  • Ectopic secretion of corticosteroids/ACTH/CRH by neoplasms (e.g., small cell lung carcinoma).
  • Activating germline mutations in the ACTH receptor (recently described).
Clinical:
  • More prevalent in females overall; pre-pubertal onset more common in boys.
  • Weight gain and "moon facies." Pigmentation appears only in the subset with increased ACTH secretion (usually a primary pituitary neoplasm); its pattern is identical to adrenal insufficiency (same ACTH/α-MSH mechanism).
The three main diagnostic tests Low-dose dexamethasone suppression test · Midnight plasma cortisol · 24-hour urinary free cortisol. Pigmentation often resolves after treating the source. Pasireotide (a somatostatin analog) is approved for treatment.

Hyperthyroidism (Graves') & Primary Biliary Cirrhosis

Pigmentation in Graves' disease?GRAVES

Melanosis is a common consequence of hyperthyroidism (Graves'), especially in dark-skinned individuals.

Number At least 40% of black patients with thyrotoxicosis may present with mucocutaneous hyperpigmentation; it is very rare in Caucasians. Pigmentation resolves after treating the thyroid abnormality.
Primary biliary cirrhosis: pigmentation, antibody, and jaundice DDx?PBC

Develops mainly in middle-aged women, from damage to small intrahepatic bile ducts. Diffuse mucocutaneous hyperpigmentation may be an early manifestation, affecting up to 47% of patients. Unknown etiology, thought autoimmune given antimitochondrial antibodies.

It may also cause generalized non-melanocytic discoloration. Jaundice is usually end-stage, caused by excess serum bilirubin (a hemoglobin breakdown product) → yellowing of skin, eyes, mucous membranes. Other jaundice causes: cirrhosis, hepatitis, neoplasia, gallstones, congenital disorders, infection.

DDx of apparent yellowing Carotenemia (excess β-carotene) and lycopenemia (excess lycopene from tomatoes/vegetables). Key difference: neither affects the oral mucosal tissues (true jaundice does).

Survival: normal in stage 1–2; ~10–15 years in advanced stages.

Vitamin B12 (Cobalamin) deficiency

Oral/systemic manifestations of B12 deficiency & its pigmentation?B12

B12 deficiency causes systemic features: megaloblastic anemia, neurologic signs, and cutaneous/oral manifestations including generalized burning, erythema, and mucosal atrophy.

Diffuse mucocutaneous hyperpigmentation is a rare, poorly recognized complication, and it is suggestive of Addison's disease — an important differentiator. It resolves once B12 levels are restored.

Link "Addison-like diffuse pigmentation + megaloblastic anemia + neurologic symptoms" → think B12 deficiency, not adrenal disease.

Peutz–Jeghers syndrome

Peutz–Jeghers: gene, features, and clinical picture?PJS

An autosomal dominant disease associated with mutations in the STK11/LKB1 tumor-suppressor gene.

Characteristic triad:
  • Intestinal polyposis — hamartomatous.
  • Cancer susceptibility.
  • Multiple small pigmented macules of lips, perioral skin, hands, feet.
  • Macules resemble ephelides, usually < 0.5 cm, but their intensity is NOT influenced by sun exposure (a golden differentiator from true freckles).
  • Similar lesions may appear on the anterior tongue, buccal and labial mucosae.
  • Lip/perioral pigmentation is highly distinctive but not pathognomonic.
  • Histology: increased basilar melanin without increased melanocyte number.
  • Management: surveillance and treatment of hamartomatous polyps.

Café au lait pigmentation

Café au lait spots: what do multiple spots indicate? Histology?CAFÉ AU LAIT

Solitary idiopathic café au lait spots occur in the general population, but multiple spots often indicate an underlying genetic disorder: Neurofibromatosis type I, McCune–Albright syndrome, and Noonan's syndrome.

  • Appearance: tan/brown irregular macules of variable size anywhere on skin; rare similar oral macules.
  • NF1: autosomal dominant, mutation/deletion of the NF1 gene on chromosome 17, with multiple neurofibromas. Size/number/age-at-onset of the spots is diagnostically important.
  • Highly characteristic NF1 signs: axillary/inguinal freckling (Crowe's sign) and iris pigmented lesions (Lisch nodules).
Fine microscopic point (examiners love it) Compared with adjacent skin, genetic café au lait spots show basilar melanosis without increased melanocyte number, but the melanocytes contain giant melanosomes (macromelanosomes) visible under light microscopy. Compared with similar lesions in normal patients, genetic spots do show increased melanocyte numbers.

HIV/AIDS-associated melanosis

Causes and features of HIV/AIDS-associated melanosis?HIV/AIDS

Diffuse or multifocal pigmentation is frequently described in HIV-seropositive patients. Possible causes:

  • Drugs (antifungal, antiretroviral).
  • Adrenocortical destruction by virulent infectious organisms.
  • In some patients (even newly diagnosed) with no adrenal disease or drug intake, the cause is undetermined; studies suggest melanosis may be a genuine, potentially late-stage clinical manifestation of HIV/AIDS.
  • Significant correlation between pigment and low CD4 count (< 200 cells/μL).
  • Immune dysregulation increases α-MSH secretion from the anterior pituitary → more melanin synthesis.
  • History of progressive hyperpigmentation of skin, nails, mucous membranes; buccal mucosa is the most frequently affected site, but gingiva, palate, tongue may be involved.

Treatment of mucocutaneous melanosis & exogenous ochronosis

General treatment principles for melanosis, and what is exogenous ochronosis?TREATMENT

General principle: focally pigmented lesions warrant removal for diagnostic and therapeutic purposes. For multifocal/diffuse pigmentation (except neoplasia), surgery is less of an option. Drug-induced and exogenously stimulated pigmentation may subside after withdrawing the agent, or may be persistent/permanent — causing social and psychological stress.

Options for gingival pigmentation:
  • Different-thickness flaps, gingivectomy, cryotherapy, electrosurgery, bur abrasion, scalpel scraping.
  • Laser therapy is effective, but benefits may be temporary with partial recurrence in ≥ 20%.
First-line pharmacologic:

Topical bleaching creams. Single agents (azelaic acid or hydroquinone) are used, but dual/triple combination is more commonly recommended: 4% hydroquinone + retinoic acid 0.05% + fluocinolone acetonide 0.01%, effective in > 90%.

Exogenous ochronosis (a bleaching complication) Intense cutaneous hyperpigmentation (± atrophic striae, coarsening, coalesced black papules), more common in black women after long-term bleaching therapy. Due to accumulation of a yellow-brown pigment (NOT melanin) in the dermis where the cream was applied (often the face); may be permanent. Effective treatment: Q-switched Nd:YAG laser. Novel tyrosinase inhibitors remain experimental.

Depigmentation — Vitiligo

Vitiligo: pathogenesis, clinical types, oral involvement, and management?VITILIGO

A relatively common acquired autoimmune disease of hypomelanosis via melanocyte destruction. Affects 0.5–2% of the world population with no gender or racial preference.

Multifactorial pathogenesis (genetic + environmental): autoimmunity, cytotoxicity, genetics, metabolic/oxidative stress. A vitiligo-susceptibility SNP is also linked to other autoimmune diseases: type 1 diabetes, SLE, rheumatoid arthritis.

Modern classification:
  • Nonsegmental: multiple achromic patches, remitting-relapsing.
  • Segmental: dermatomeric distribution, rapid onset, usually not progressive.
  • Unclassified/undetermined.
  • Onset at any age, most often the 2nd–3rd decade; more apparent in darker skin but occurs in all races.
  • May arise during immunotherapy for melanoma and may then indicate a better prognosis.
  • Vitiligo rarely affects intraoral mucosa, but hypomelanosis of the lips (inner/outer) and perioral skin is seen in up to 20%.
  • Microscopy: melanocyte destruction by antigen-specific T cells, complete loss of basal melanin; Fontana-Masson stain confirms absence.
Management (aim: repigmentation):
  • Topical corticosteroids, topical calcineurin inhibitors, narrow-band UVB, PUVA.
  • Labial vitiligo is more resistant: lips lack hair follicles, so there is no melanocyte reservoir to stimulate.
  • Hence surgery may be the only option: autologous epithelial grafts, split-thickness skin grafts (highest repigmentation success), punch grafting, micropigmentation.

Part 4 · Hemoglobin & Iron-associated Pigmentation

Big picture: how do blood/iron pigments differ from melanin lesions?OVERVIEW

Until now most lesions were melanin-based. Here the pigment comes from hemoglobin and iron. When blood extravasates, hemoglobin degrades to hemosiderin — an iron-rich brown pigment — so the lesion is red first, then brown within a few days. This time-dependent color evolution (red → brown) is the key that distinguishes these from the stable color of melanocytic lesions.

Ecchymosis

Ecchymosis: evolution, DDx, and systemic associations?ECCHYMOSIS

Traumatic ecchymosis is common on lips and face but uncommon within the oral mucosa, except with blunt-force trauma or intubation.

  • Immediately: erythrocyte extravasation appears as a bright red macule, or a swelling if a hematoma forms.
  • Turns brown within a few days after hemoglobin degrades to hemosiderin.
  • If the patient recalls trauma: observe for two weeks; it should resolve (a useful time test).
  • Anticoagulant patients may show oral ecchymosis (esp. buccal mucosa/tongue traumatized while chewing).
  • Also seen in liver cirrhosis, leukemia, and end-stage renal disease on dialysis.
When to test coagulation For spontaneous ecchymoses (no trauma): obtain bleeding time, PT, PTT, INR to explore coagulation defects.

Purpura / Petechiae

Distinguish purpura from petechiae; where do oral ones appear?PURPURA

Capillary hemorrhages appear red initially and turn brown in a few days once extravasated cells lyse and degrade to hemosiderin — same logic as ecchymosis but smaller.

The distinction (size only — essentially semantic) Petechiae: pinpoint or slightly larger. Purpura: multiple small 2–4 mm collections of extravasated blood.
  • Causes: trauma, viral, or systemic disease.
  • Petechiae from platelet deficiency/aggregation disorders are usually not limited to the mouth — they occur on skin too.
  • Viral disease is more commonly associated with oral than cutaneous petechiae; the soft palate is the most common oral site.
  • If trauma is suspected: stop the offending activity and observe two weeks; failure to resolve suggests a hemorrhagic diathesis, persistent infection, or systemic disease, and labs are needed.

Hemochromatosis

Hemochromatosis: nature, complications, and oral manifestations?HEMOCHROMATOSIS

A chronic progressive disease of excessive iron deposition (usually hemosiderin) in liver and other organs. Forms: idiopathic, neonatal, transfusional, and heritable.

Systemic complications:

Liver cirrhosis, diabetes, anemia, heart failure, hypertension, and skin bronzing — cutaneous pigmentation in over 90% of patients regardless of etiology.

Oral manifestations:
  • Primary: blue-gray to brown pigmentation mainly of the palate and gingiva.
  • Early on, pigmentation may be more from basilar melanosis than iron pigment.
  • Iron deposition in the adrenal cortex can cause hypoadrenocorticism and ACTH hypersecretion → addisonian-type changes (link back to Part 3!).
  • Later: pigmentation is usually from hemosiderosis + melanosis.
Easy diagnostic tool A lower labial gland biopsy is an easy, effective method: it shows increased basal-layer melanin plus golden/brown hemosiderin scattered through the submucosal and salivary gland tissues.

Part 5 · Exogenous Pigmentation (Tattoos)

Amalgam tattoo

Amalgam tattoo: cause, sites, histology, and when to biopsy?AMALGAM

Amalgam tattoo is the most common pigmented lesion of the oral mucosa. By definition iatrogenic: accidental deposition of amalgam restorative material into submucosal tissue.

Clinical:
  • Found in 1–3% of the general population; small, asymptomatic, macular, bluish-gray or black.
  • Any surface, but most common: gingiva, alveolar mucosa, buccal mucosa, floor of mouth.
  • Often near teeth with large amalgams or crowned teeth (that likely had amalgams), around apices of endodontically treated teeth with retrograde restorations or silver points, and around healed extraction sites.
  • Amalgam tattoo of head/neck skin can occur in dentists — an occupational hazard from failing to use facial protection.
Microscopy:

Fine brown granular stippling of collagen fibers, with a particular affinity for vessel walls and nerve fibers, and little/no inflammation. Large aggregates may cause a foreign-body giant-cell granulomatous reaction, though a mild-to-moderate lymphocytic infiltrate is more common.

When to biopsy (a key decision point) Fine particles usually aren't radiographically visible; large ones may be. Biopsy if: no radiographic amalgam, the lesion is not near a restored tooth, or the lesion appears suddenly. Innocent, radiographically documented tattoos don't always need removal. DDx: melanotic macule, nevus, melanoma.

Other restorative materials can also cause pigmentation; metal from most cast alloys can be detected in adjacent tissues. Titanium is associated with skin pigmentation around orthopedic implants, suggesting dental implants may be a potential source of exogenous oral pigmentation.

Graphite & Ornamental tattoos

Graphite tattoos and ornamental tattoos — key points?GRAPHITE/ART

Graphite tattoos: an unusual source of focal exogenous pigmentation, most common on the palate and gingiva, representing traumatic implantation of graphite from a pencil. May be indistinguishable from amalgam tattoo (solitary gray/black macule).

  • The injury often occurs in childhood and may not be recalled → biopsy is often warranted.
  • Microscopically, particles resemble amalgam.
  • In cosmetic areas: removal + an autogenous connective tissue graft gives a highly esthetic outcome.

Ornamental tattoos: mucosal tattoos as lettering or artwork, increasingly common. Amateur inks are permanent, made of simple carbon particles from various sources (burnt wood/plastic/paper, India ink, pen ink, plant matter). Q-switched laser therapy has been used successfully for removal.

High-Yield Recap
  • Most common oral pigmented lesion = amalgam tattoo; most common diffuse pigmentation = physiologic.
  • Palate = most common site of oral melanoma and the worst prognosis; 10-year survival = 0%.
  • One-third of oral melanomas are amelanotic → never rely on color alone.
  • ACTH & α-MSH share POMC; first 13 amino acids of ACTH = α-MSH → pigment in Addison's and ACTH-high Cushing's.
  • Peutz–Jeghers macules are not affected by sun (differentiates from freckles).
  • PBC → antimitochondrial antibodies; B12 deficiency → Addison-like pigmentation.
  • Hemosiderin (red→brown) for ecchymosis/petechiae; lower labial gland biopsy for hemochromatosis.
  • Vitiligo → Fontana-Masson confirms melanin absence; labial vitiligo is resistant (no hair follicles).
  • Triple bleaching combo: 4% hydroquinone + tretinoin 0.05% + fluocinolone 0.01%.
  • Ochronosis = bleaching complication (pigment is NOT melanin) → Q-switched Nd:YAG.

Glossary · Key Terms

Melanocyte
Dendritic cell in the basal layer producing melanin within melanosomes and transferring it to keratinocytes.
Melanin incontinence
Leakage of melanin from the basal layer into connective tissue, engulfed by melanophages.
Radial vs. vertical growth phase
Superficial lateral spread (better prognosis) vs. deep vertical invasion (worse) in melanoma.
Breslow thickness / Clark level
Two melanoma prognostic criteria; Breslow (depth in mm) is the strongest.
Amelanosis
Absent or minimal pigment in a melanocytic lesion (occurs in one-third of oral melanomas).
α-MSH / ACTH / POMC
Hormones derived from the POMC precursor; elevated in Addison's and some Cushing's, driving pigmentation.
Antimitochondrial antibodies
Autoimmune marker of primary biliary cirrhosis (PBC).
Macromelanosomes
Giant melanosomes seen in genetic café au lait spots under light microscopy.
Hemosiderin
Iron-rich brown pigment from hemoglobin breakdown; cause of the brown color in ecchymosis and hemochromatosis.
Fontana-Masson stain
Histochemical stain for melanin; confirms its absence in vitiligo.
Exogenous ochronosis
Cutaneous hyperpigmentation (pigment NOT melanin) after prolonged bleaching; treated with Q-switched Nd:YAG laser.
Oral submucous fibrosis
Preneoplastic condition from areca-nut chewing, with pigmentation + fibrosis (contrast with non-preneoplastic smoker's melanosis).

MCQ · 20 Questions

1The single most common intraoral site for primary oral malignant melanoma is the:
Answer: D. The palate is the most common single site of oral melanoma and also carries the worst prognosis.
2The 10-year survival rate of oral mucosal malignant melanoma is approximately:
Answer: C. 10-year survival = 0% (5-year survival 15–40%).
3Up to one-third of oral melanomas may show little or no pigmentation. This is termed:
Answer: B. Amelanosis widens the differential and underscores the need for biopsy.
4Which is the MOST common cause of multifocal/diffuse oral mucosal pigmentation?
Answer: A. Physiologic pigmentation is the most common; it appears in childhood, not de novo in adults.
5The chief drugs implicated in drug-induced melanosis are the:
Answer: D. Antimalarials are chief, followed by phenothiazines, contraceptives, and cytotoxics.
6Which statement about smoker's melanosis is TRUE?
Answer: C. Smoker's melanosis is not preneoplastic, unlike oral submucous fibrosis. Snuff is not associated with increased melanosis.
7Melasma is MOST characteristically induced by:
Answer: B. The estrogen + progesterone combination (with sun) induces it; estrogen alone does not.
8In Addison's disease, the first 13 amino acids of ACTH are identical to:
Answer: D. ACTH and α-MSH derive from POMC; the identity explains the hyperpigmentation.
9A 9:00 a.m. serum cortisol level diagnostic of adrenal insufficiency is:
Answer: A. < 100 nmol/L at 9 a.m. is diagnostic, with hyponatremia and hyperkalemia.
10Which test is NOT one of the three main tests for Cushing's syndrome?
Answer: C. Antimitochondrial antibodies belong to PBC, not Cushing's.
11Primary biliary cirrhosis is characterized by the presence of:
Answer: B. PBC is autoimmune with antimitochondrial antibodies, mostly in middle-aged women.
12Carotenemia and lycopenemia differ from true jaundice in that they:
Answer: D. They spare the oral mucosal tissues, unlike true jaundice.
13Peutz–Jeghers syndrome is associated with mutation of which gene?
Answer: A. Autosomal dominant STK11/LKB1 mutation, with intestinal polyps and sun-independent macules.
14Multiple café au lait spots most classically suggest:
Answer: C. Multiple spots suggest NF1 (also McCune–Albright and Noonan). NF1 signs: Crowe's sign and Lisch nodules.
15HIV/AIDS-associated melanosis correlates significantly with a CD4 count:
Answer: B. Significant correlation with CD4 < 200; buccal mucosa is the most affected site.
16Exogenous ochronosis results from accumulation of:
Answer: D. A yellow-brown, non-melanin pigment accumulates in the dermis after prolonged bleaching; treat with Q-switched Nd:YAG laser.
17Which histochemical stain confirms the absence of melanin in vitiligo?
Answer: A. Fontana-Masson confirms the absence of melanin in the basal layer.
18In ecchymosis, the brown discoloration appearing within a few days is due to degradation of hemoglobin to:
Answer: C. Hemoglobin degrades to hemosiderin, producing the brown color.
19The MOST common pigmented lesion of the oral mucosa is:
Answer: B. Amalgam tattoo is the most common oral pigmented lesion — iatrogenic, gray-blue or black.
20An easy, effective biopsy method to diagnose hemochromatosis is a biopsy of the:
Answer: D. A lower labial salivary gland biopsy shows increased basal melanin plus scattered golden/brown hemosiderin.

خلاصة بسيطة بالعربي

موضوع اليوم هو الآفات الصبغية للغشاء المخاطي الفموي. أهم النقاط باختصار:

  • أي آفة صبغية بؤرية منفردة ومستمرة تحتاج خزعة لاستبعاد الميلانوما.
  • الميلانوما الفموية: الحنك أشيع موقع وأسوأ إنذار، وبقيا ١٠ سنوات = صفر٪، وثلثها بلا لون واضح (amelanotic).
  • التصبّغ المنتشر: أشيع سبب فسيولوجي (منذ الطفولة)؛ الظهور المفاجئ عند البالغ علامة إنذار.
  • أسباب جهازية: أديسون وكوشينغ (بآلية ACTH/α-MSH)، غريفز، تشمّع الصفراء الأولي (أضداد ميتوكوندريا)، عوز B12 (يشبه أديسون)، Peutz–Jeghers (بقع لا تتأثّر بالشمس)، café au lait/NF1، وHIV مع CD4 منخفض.
  • الدم والحديد: كدمات وحبرات (تتحوّل للبني بسبب الهيموسيديرين)، وhemochromatosis (خزعة الغدة الشفوية السفلية).
  • خارجي: وشم الأملغم أشيع آفة صبغية فموية، ثم الغرافيت والوشوم الزخرفية.
  • البهاق: مرض مناعي ذاتي، صبغة Fontana-Masson تؤكّد غياب الميلانين.

بقية الملف (البطاقات، الجداول، الأسئلة) كلها بالإنكليزي للمذاكرة والامتحان.
