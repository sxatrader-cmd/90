Part 1 · Metal / Drug Pigmentation & Hairy Tongue

Medicinal metal-induced pigmentation

Gold, silver, and bismuth: what pigmentation do they cause?METALS

Various metallic compounds were historically used to treat systemic disease. Gold therapy is now less used since methotrexate for rheumatoid arthritis; colloidal silver persists in "complementary/alternative" medicine.

Two named discolorations Silver → argyria: generalized blue-gray discoloration. Gold → chrysiasis: blue-gray or purple. Both may be persistent or permanent even after stopping the substance.
  • Systemic gold therapy can also cause oral lichenoid eruptions.
  • Silver nitrate (used to cauterize recurrent aphthous stomatitis) and zinc oxide (in sunblock) can cause focal mucocutaneous pigmentation; zinc-oxide sunblock on chapped lips may cause hyperpigmentation.
  • Medicinal silver pigment appears as brown/black particulate matter dispersed in the connective tissue.
Bismuth black tongue:

Generalized black tongue can result from chewing bismuth subsalicylate antacid tablets — due to deposition of actual pigment (bismuth sulfide) without lingual changes. Unlike black hairy tongue (elongated filiform papillae + hyperkeratosis + bacterial colonization). Treatment: stop the antacid and clean the tongue (curative). Note: bismuth subsalicylate can also cause typical black hairy tongue.

Heavy metal pigmentation

What causes heavy-metal oral pigmentation?HEAVY METALS

Diffuse oral pigmentation may follow ingestion of heavy metals. It remains an occupational/health hazard for people working in certain industrial plants and those living in/around such facilities.

Common environmental sources include paints, old plumbing, and seafood.

Drug-induced pigmentation (minocycline & others)

Minocycline pigmentation: teeth, bone, and soft tissue effects?MINOCYCLINE

Minocycline (a tetracycline derivative used for acne) is a relatively common cause of drug-induced, non-melanin oral pigmentation.

  • Like tetracycline, it can pigment developing teeth, but most patients take it in early adulthood.
  • Chronic use → metabolites incorporated into normal bone: teeth may look normal but surrounding bone may appear green, blue, or black, making the palatal/alveolar mucosa look diffusely discolored. Roots appear green; developing roots tend to be black.
  • It can also pigment oral soft tissues, skin, and nails — gray, brown, or black, often patchy or diffuse.
Pathology & course:

Biopsy may show basilar melanosis, but more commonly fine brown/golden particle aggregates in the submucosa, often intracellular within macrophages — likely a precipitated drug metabolite, not true melanin. Mucosal discoloration usually subsides within months after stopping; bone pigment may persist indefinitely. Severe cutaneous cases respond to Alexandrite 755-nm laser.

Other drugs: methacycline (older tetracycline derivative) and imatinib (a tyrosine kinase inhibitor for chronic myeloid leukemia) can also induce mucosal pigmentation.

Hairy tongue

Hairy tongue: cause, appearance, and treatment?HAIRY TONGUE

A relatively common condition of unknown etiology; a change in oral flora with chronic antibiotic therapy may contribute. It involves the dorsal tongue, especially the middle and posterior third; children are rarely affected.

  • Filiform papillae are elongated (like fine hairs) and become pigmented by colonizing chromogenic bacteria → white, green, brown, or black.
  • Foods/drinks/confectionaries contribute; smoking tobacco or crack cocaine is associated with black hairy tongue.
  • Drug associations: tetracycline, linezolid, olanzapine, bismuth, erlotinib, and psychotropics.
  • Microscopy: markedly elongated, hyperplastic filiform papillae with hyperkeratosis + prominent superficial microbial colonization; no other epithelial/connective-tissue pathology.

Treatment: brush the tongue or use a tongue scraper, and limit color-forming foods/drinks until it resolves. Because the cause is often undetermined, it may recur.

Part 2 · Neuromuscular Overview & Cerebrovascular Disease

What are neuromuscular disorders, and how are they classified here?OVERVIEW

Neuromuscular disorders affect both nerve and muscle tissue, weakening the body's voluntary muscles through interrupted communication between the nervous system and muscles. They can usually be managed to improve quality/length of life but are incurable.

Symptoms: muscle weakness, rigidity, loss of muscular control, numbness, tingling, twitching, spasms, and muscle/limb pain.

Classification covered today Cerebrovascular disease · Multiple sclerosis · Alzheimer's disease · Seizure disorders · Parkinson's disease · Myasthenia gravis (plus Bell's palsy).

Cerebrovascular disease (stroke / CVA)

Define stroke/CVA, TIA, and RIND. What time thresholds separate them?DEFINITIONS

Cerebrovascular disease = all disorders that damage the blood vessels supplying the brain, impairing cerebral circulation and producing neurologic damage.

  • Stroke / CVA: a sudden impairment in cerebral circulation causing death or a focal neurologic deficit lasting more than 24 hours. Complete cessation of flow can cause irreversible infarct within 3–4 minutes.
  • TIA (transient ischemic attack): a "mini-stroke" — reversible, acute, short-duration focal deficit from transient (reversible within 24 hours) localized cerebral ischemia.
  • RIND (reversible ischemic neurologic defect): reversible focal deficit from transient ischemia, but deficits last more than 24 hours.
Types of CVA by cause, and the three ischemic stroke syndromes?TYPES
By cause:
  • Atherosclerosis (85%) → ischemia/infarction from thromboembolic events and vessel occlusion.
  • Cerebral hemorrhage (15%) → hemorrhagic infarction, most often from hypertension, trauma, substance abuse, or aneurysmal rupture.
Three ischemic stroke syndromes:
  • Small vessel (lacunar): obstruction of small (< 5 mm) penetrating arterioles. Age + uncontrolled hypertension are the greatest predisposers. Unilateral motor/sensory deficit without visual-field/consciousness/language change; prognosis fair-to-good, resolving over 4–6 weeks.
  • Large vessel (cerebral infarction): extensive downstream ischemia, usually a thromboembolic event along the internal carotid/cerebral arteries; emboli often from the heart (after MI or in atrial fibrillation). Hypertension is key (esp. at the carotid bifurcation). High-level brain functions affected; prognosis poor.
  • Brainstem infarction: occlusion of vessels supplying the brainstem; deficits range from motor/sensory loss to death if respiratory centers are affected.
Prognostic fact About 30% of people with a history of TIA have a completed stroke within 5 years.
Clinical manifestations, diagnosis, and treatment of stroke?DX & RX

Clinical: varies with size/location — sensory and motor deficits, extraocular/eye-movement changes (paresis), visual defects, sudden headache, altered mental status, dizziness, nausea, seizures, impaired speech/hearing, and neurocognitive deficits (memory, reasoning, concentration).

Diagnosis:
  • Non-contrast CT is the first-line imaging — intracranial hemorrhage must be excluded before thrombolysis. MRI gives greater anatomic detail/sensitivity for early infarction.
  • Labs: CBC, metabolic panel, urinalysis, coagulation profile; when indicated blood culture, echocardiography, lumbar puncture; plus electrolytes, thyroid, glucose, cholesterol, CRP.
Treatment:
  • Timeliness is critical. TIAs/RIND: reduce hypertension (lifestyle + medical therapy) and antiplatelet/anticoagulant drugs.
  • Acute stroke: reduce bleeding/thromboembolic occlusion, reduce edema/neurotoxicity, surgery (revascularization, hemorrhage control).
  • Once hemorrhage excluded: IV tissue plasminogen activator (t-PA) improves reperfusion and reduces disability.
  • After a completed stroke: prevent further damage (risk-factor reduction) + rehabilitation (speech, physical therapy). Intracranial hemorrhage = medical emergency (airway, ICU).
Oral health considerations after stroke (bleeding, anesthesia, timing)?DENTAL

Post-stroke oral problems: masticatory/facial muscle paralysis, impaired touch/taste, diminished gag reflex, dysphagia → poor intake, poor nutrition, weight loss, choking. Reduced dexterity worsens oral hygiene → higher caries/periodontal risk.

Key management points
  • Risk of a second stroke is highest in the first 90 days — optimal monitoring before invasive treatment.
  • Antiplatelets/anticoagulants are common: aspirin, LMW heparin, less commonly warfarin. At therapeutic doses (warfarin INR ≤ 3.5) they rarely need modification for routine/minor oral surgery.
  • Obtain coagulation values (PT, INR, APTT, bleeding time) before invasive work.
  • NSAIDs may increase bleeding risk and reduce aspirin's protective effect.
  • Epinephrine-containing local anesthetics are NOT contraindicated but use judiciously (cardiovascular guidelines); do NOT use epinephrine-containing retraction cord. Monitor BP each visit.
  • Xerostomia is a common medication side effect → higher caries rate.

Weigh bleeding risk against thrombosis risk — stopping antiplatelets/warfarin may precipitate a thrombotic event (DVT, stroke).

Cavernous sinus thrombosis

Cavernous sinus thrombosis: source, signs, pathogen, and treatment?CST

A rare but severe (possibly fatal) complication, usually secondary to dental, nasal, or ocular infections. Maxillary dental infections can spread to the cavernous sinus via emissary veins; venous propagation runs facial vein → ophthalmic vein → cavernous sinus.

Signs/symptoms:

Severe headache with tearing, periorbital swelling/redness, drooping eyelids, inability to move the eye, high fever, facial pain/numbness, vision loss/double vision, altered mental status. Classic acute signs: exophthalmos, periorbital edema, retinal vein thrombosis, and involvement of the ophthalmic division of the trigeminal nerve plus trochlear/abducent nerves → ptosis, dilated pupils, lost corneal reflexes.

Pathogen & treatment Staphylococcus aureus is the most common pathogen (~70%). Immediate antibiotics + remove the infection source. Empiric: vancomycin + a 3rd-generation cephalosporin (ceftriaxone); add IV metronidazole if dental/sinus infection is suspected; antifungals only for confirmed invasive fungal infection. High-dose IV antibiotics for ≥ 2 weeks beyond clinical resolution.

Part 3 · Multiple Sclerosis, Alzheimer's & Parkinson's

Multiple sclerosis (MS)

MS: pathology, etiology, and hallmark signs?MS

Chronic neurologic disease of multiple areas of CNS white-matter inflammation, demyelination, and gliosis (scarring). Loss of myelin slows/blocks impulse propagation. More frequent in women; average onset in the 4th decade; presents as recurrent attacks.

Etiology:
  • Autoimmune basis (activated T cells + autoantibodies to glycoproteins in lesions).
  • Environmental/infectious triggers: Epstein–Barr virus and HHV-6 most implicated; also measles, mumps, rubella, parainfluenza. Genetic influence too.
Signs (site-dependent — optic chiasm, brainstem, cerebellum, spinal cord):
  • > 60% have visual disturbance from CN II demyelination; vision loss over days with partial recovery within a month. Also color blindness, diplopia (CN III, IV, VI).
  • Uhthoff's sign: rapid vision loss after body-temperature rise (e.g., exercise).
  • Lhermitte's symptom: electric-shock sensations on neck flexion radiating down the back/legs.
  • Weakness/paresthesia with increased deep tendon reflexes; bladder dysfunction, ataxia, vertigo, incoordination; chronic exacerbations and remissions.
Diagnosis & treatment:

Mainly clinical; no definitive lab test, but MRI shows demyelinating plaques in > 90–95% (hyperintense). Evoked potentials abnormal in up to 90%; CSF shows increased total protein and mononuclear WBCs. Treatment: high-dose IV corticosteroids (IV methylprednisolone) for attacks; long-term immunosuppressants (azathioprine, methotrexate for chronic progressive); interferon-β agents reduce attacks/lesions.

Oral considerations Trigeminal neuralgia may be an initial manifestation of MS in up to 3% (may lack trigger zones, continuous lower-intensity pain). Also V2/V3 neuropathy, mental-nerve numbness of lip/chin, myokymia (flickering facial contractions), facial weakness, and scanning dysarthria. Avoid elective dental treatment during acute exacerbations.

Alzheimer's disease (AD)

Alzheimer's: stages, diagnosis, and treatment?ALZHEIMER'S

Dementia = acquired deterioration in cognitive abilities impairing daily function; memory is the most common ability lost. Familial AD is autosomal dominant, onset typically before 65.

Three stages (a continuum):
  • Preclinical AD — before cognitive changes (research use).
  • Cognitive impairment due to AD — mild noticeable memory changes not yet interfering with daily life.
  • Dementia due to AD — changes in ≥ 2 cognitive/behavioral domains interfering with everyday function.

Initial sign is retrograde amnesia (declining episodic memory). End-stage: rigid, mute, incontinent, bedridden, possible generalized seizures; death often from malnutrition, heart disease, pulmonary emboli, or secondary infection.

Diagnosis:

Preclinical uses biomarkers (Aβ deposition; downstream neurodegeneration — elevated CSF tau + brain atrophy on MRI). Clinical diagnosis: history + exam showing progressive cognitive decline absent other causes. "Possible AD" = meets dementia criteria but has another contributing illness (hypothyroidism, cerebrovascular disease, vitamin deficiency, depression). EEG: generalized slowing. MRI/CT: dilated lateral ventricles, widened cortical sulci (esp. temporal).

Treatment (no cure — slows progression):
  • Cholinesterase inhibitors — standard of care for mild-to-moderate AD.
  • Memantine (NMDA-receptor antagonist) — moderate-to-severe AD; better combined with cholinesterase inhibitors.
  • SSRIs for depression; antipsychotics for aggression/psychosis (later stages).
Oral considerations Higher risk of coronal/root caries, periodontal infection, TMJ problems, orofacial pain. Complete restorative care early (cooperation declines with cognition); aggressive prevention + 3-month recall. Cholinesterase inhibitors → sialorrhea; antidepressants/antipsychotics → xerostomia.

Parkinsonism

Parkinsonism: cardinal features, oral signs, and drugs?PARKINSON

A neurodegenerative disorder characterized by rigidity, tremor, bradykinesia, and impaired postural reflexes (postural instability). The most common form is Parkinson's disease (paralysis agitans); also postencephalitic and post-traumatic forms.

Head & neck / oral signs:
  • Typical mask-like facial appearance with infrequent blinking and lack of expression (from bradykinesia).
  • Muscle rigidity → swallowing difficulty and saliva drooling; speech affected; mandibular tremor → chewing difficulty (esp. with removable appliances).
  • Purposeless chewing, grinding, sucking movements complicate dental treatment.
Treatment:
  • Often not required early. Mild symptoms → amantadine.
  • Anticholinergics help tremor/rigidity more than bradykinesia (many side effects).
  • Levodopa (dopamine precursor crossing the blood-brain barrier) improves all major features.

Part 4 · Bell's Palsy & Myasthenia Gravis

Bell's palsy

Bell's palsy: nature, cause, features, and treatment?BELL'S PALSY

Bell's palsy = a unilateral paresis of the facial nerve (CN VII), attributed to an inflammatory reaction of the nerve. A relationship exists with isolation of herpes simplex virus 1 from nerve tissue.

Presentation:
  • Begins with slight pain around one ear, then abrupt paralysis of that side of the face.
  • The eye on the affected side stays open, the corner of the mouth drops, drooling occurs.
  • Masseter weakness → food retained in buccal/labial folds; forehead creases flattened.
  • Impaired blinking → corneal ulceration risk.
Symptoms:

Rapid onset (hours–days) of mild weakness to total unilateral facial paralysis, facial droop, drooling, jaw/ear pain on the affected side, hyperacusis, headache, decreased taste, altered tears/saliva. Linked viruses: HSV, VZV (chickenpox/shingles), EBV, CMV, adenovirus, rubella, mumps, influenza B.

Diagnosis & treatment:
  • No specific test — clinical (ask to close eyes, lift brow, show teeth, frown). Rule out stroke, infection, Lyme disease, tumor. EMG confirms/grades nerve damage; MRI/CT to exclude a mass/fracture.
  • Corticosteroids (prednisone) — best if started within several days of onset. Antivirals alone show no benefit vs placebo; valacyclovir may be added to prednisone in severe cases. Physical therapy prevents contractures. Decompression surgery is no longer recommended.

Myasthenia gravis

Myasthenia gravis: mechanism, signs, treatment, and dental cautions?MYASTHENIA

Disease of progressive muscular weakness on exertion, from a disorder at the neuromuscular junction. Autoimmune: autoantibodies destroy acetylcholine receptor sites, preventing nerve-impulse transmission to muscle.

  • Initial signs often in cranial-nerve-innervated areas (frequently eye muscles): ptosis, diplopia, difficulty chewing/swallowing, respiratory difficulty, limb weakness.
  • Oral/facial: facial muscles of expression involved; tongue edema; easy fatigability — cannot finish chewing a food bolus.
Treatment:

Anticholinesterases (neostigmine, pyridostigmine), thymectomy, long-term corticosteroids/immunosuppressants.

Dental management A respiratory crisis may develop from the disease or overmedication — treat in a hospital with intubation available; keep the airway clear (aspiration risk), use suction + rubber dam. Avoid drugs affecting the neuromuscular junction: narcotics, tranquilizers, barbiturates; and antibiotics like tetracycline, streptomycin, sulfonamides, clindamycin.

Part 5 · Seizure Disorders & Epilepsy

Define seizure and epilepsy; what are the three major seizure categories?CLASSIFICATION

A seizure = a paroxysmal event from abnormal, excessive, hypersynchronous discharges of CNS neurons. Epilepsy = a group of disorders with recurrent seizure activity.

Three major categories:
  • Focal (partial): originates within networks limited to one hemisphere. Simple partial seizures (discrete cortical locus, e.g., motor cortex) generally have no impaired consciousness — clonic activity (rapid jerks) ± somatosensory, visual, auditory, olfactory, gustatory phenomena.
  • Generalized: arise from both hemispheres simultaneously (abnormal neuronal excitability). Includes absence, tonic-clonic, atypical absence, atonic, myoclonic.
  • Unknown: cannot be classified as focal or generalized.
Grand mal vs. petit mal — distinguish them.GRAND vs PETIT
Tonic-clonic (grand mal) Most common type — ~90% of epileptics experience it alone or combined. Begins with an aura (epigastric discomfort, an emotion, or a hallucination of hearing/vision/smell), then unconsciousness ± a cry, then tonic muscle spasm (~30 s, apnea → cyanosis), followed by a clonic phase (convulsive jerks, incontinence, tongue biting).
Absence (petit mal) Second most common; sudden brief lapses of consciousness WITHOUT loss of body tone, few/no clonic-tonic movements, no aura. Present almost exclusively in children and frequently disappear during the second decade.
Etiology of seizures by age, and diagnostic work-up?ETIOLOGY / DX
Etiology varies by age:
  • Late infancy / early childhood: febrile seizures (no CNS infection) — 3 months to 5 years, peak 18–24 months.
  • Adults (isolated non-recurrent): metabolic disturbances, toxins, drug effects, hypotension, hypoglycemia, hyponatremia, uremia, hepatic encephalopathy, overdose, withdrawal.
  • Older adults: cerebrovascular disease accounts for ~50% of new epilepsy in patients > 65.
  • More frequent with neurologic disabilities (cerebral palsy, autism).
Diagnosis:
  • History & physical (often clinical) + full neurologic exam (cranial nerves).
  • Blood: CBC, Mg, calcium, glucose (metabolic causes); toxin screen; lumbar puncture (infection).
  • Brain imaging (MRI/CT) for structural pathology.
  • EEG to classify the seizure and guide anticonvulsant choice.
Antiepileptic drugs (AEDs) and their key adverse effects?AEDs
  • Phenytoin: long half-life; gingival overgrowth, hirsutism, coarsening of facial features.
  • Carbamazepine: hepatotoxicity, leukopenia, aplastic anemia.
  • Lamotrigine: skin rash.
  • Valproic acid: for generalized tonic-clonic; bone-marrow suppression and hepatotoxicity (avoid aspirin/NSAIDs with valproic acid).
  • Others: topiramate, gabapentin, oxcarbazepine.
Stopping therapy — best chance of remaining seizure-free:
  1. Complete control for 1–5 years;
  2. Single seizure type;
  3. Normal neurologic exam (incl. intelligence);
  4. Normal EEG.

Refractory epilepsy options: surgery (limited hippocampus/amygdala removal, temporal lobectomy, hemispherectomy), vagus nerve stimulation, deep brain stimulation, responsive neurostimulation, and investigational gene therapy.

Oral health considerations in epilepsy?DENTAL
  • Refer uncontrolled patients to a hospital.
  • Patients with an implanted vagus nerve stimulator do NOT require antibiotic prophylaxis.
  • Avoid any triggers of seizure activity.
  • Prefer fixed prosthesis over removable (aspiration risk during a seizure).
  • Patients on AEDs need lab evaluation before treatment; avoid aspirin/NSAIDs with valproic acid.
  • Findings: gingival overgrowth, intraoral lesions, lip enlargement, and xerostomia (→ more caries and candidiasis). Use topical fluoride and antifungals as needed; also stomatitis, glossitis, oral ulceration.

High-Yield Recap
  • Silver → argyria (blue-gray); gold → chrysiasis (blue-gray/purple); both may be permanent.
  • Bismuth black tongue = bismuth sulfide, no papillary change (differs from black hairy tongue).
  • Minocycline pigment is a drug metabolite, not melanin; bone pigment may be permanent.
  • Stroke: 85% atherosclerotic, 15% hemorrhagic; non-contrast CT first; t-PA after excluding hemorrhage.
  • Second-stroke risk highest in the first 90 days; warfarin INR ≤ 3.5 rarely needs modification for minor oral surgery.
  • Cavernous sinus thrombosis: S. aureus ~70%; vancomycin + ceftriaxone (+ metronidazole for dental/sinus source).
  • MS: women, 4th decade; EBV/HHV-6; MRI plaques; Uhthoff's & Lhermitte's signs; TGN may be the first sign in up to 3%.
  • Alzheimer's: cholinesterase inhibitors (→ sialorrhea) + memantine; restore oral health early.
  • Parkinson's: rigidity/tremor/bradykinesia/postural instability; mask-like face; levodopa.
  • Bell's palsy: unilateral CN VII, HSV-1 link, eye stays open; steroids early.
  • Myasthenia gravis: anti-ACh-receptor antibodies; ptosis/diplopia; avoid NMJ-affecting drugs.
  • Grand mal ~90% (aura + tonic-clonic); petit mal in children (no aura, no tone loss).
  • Phenytoin → gingival overgrowth; valproic acid → avoid aspirin/NSAIDs; VNS patients need no antibiotic prophylaxis; prefer fixed prostheses.

t-PA

MS — rapid vision loss when body temperature rises (e.g., exercise).
Lhermitte's symptom
MS — electric-shock sensation on neck flexion radiating down the back/legs.

Rapid flickering facial muscle contractions from facial-nerve MS lesions.

  • MS: نساء/العقد الرابع، EBV و HHV-6، لويحات بالـMRI، علامتا Uhthoff وLhermitte، وقد يكون ألم العصب مثلث التوائم أول علامة.
  • الزهايمر: مثبّطات الكولين إستراز + ميمانتين، وأكمل العلاج السنّي مبكراً. باركنسون: تصلّب/رعاش/بطء حركة/عدم اتزان، ووجه قناعي، وليفودوبا.
  • شلل بيل: شلل نصفي للعصب الوجهي (CN VII)، مرتبط بـHSV-1، العين تبقى مفتوحة، والستيرويد مبكراً. الوهن العضلي الوبيل: أضداد لمستقبلات الأستيل كولين، تعب سريع، وتجنّب أدوية الوصل العصبي–العضلي.
  • الصرع: grand mal (~٩٠٪ مع هالة) و petit mal (أطفال، بلا هالة/بلا فقد توتر). Phenytoin يسبّب تضخّم لثة، وتجنّب الأسبرين مع valproic acid، ومرضى VNS بلا صادّ وقائي، وتفضيل التركيبات الثابتة.

بقية الملف (البطاقات، الجداول، الأسئلة) كلها بالإنكليزي للمذاكرة والامتحان.
